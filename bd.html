Class 10 Biology Lab Manual & Simple Theory

Easy-to-understand Class 10 textbook notes, procedure, and observation chart

1.1 Aim of the Experiment

To determine the ABO blood group (A, B, AB, or O) and Rh factor (Rh+ or Rh−) of a human blood sample collected using a sterile lancet by observing agglutination (clumping) on a glass slide.

Method: Slide Agglutination Test • Time Required: 20–25 Minutes

1.2 Apparatus & Reagents Required

Instruments & Glassware
  • • Sterile Disposable Blood Lancet (for safe finger prick)
  • • 70% Alcohol Swab & sterile dry cotton wool
  • • Clean dry Glass Microscope Slide (divided into 3 circles)
  • • 3 separate Plastic Toothpicks / Mixing Sticks
  • • Compound Light Microscope (optional, to confirm clumping)
Blood Grouping Reagents (Antisera)
  • Anti-A Serum (Blue color): Contains antibodies against Antigen A.
  • Anti-B Serum (Yellow color): Contains antibodies against Antigen B.
  • Anti-D / Anti-Rh Serum (Colorless): Contains antibodies against Rh factor.

1.3 Class 10 Principle & Simple Theory

Human blood groups were discovered by Karl Landsteiner. Every person's Red Blood Cells (RBCs) carry special proteins on their outer surface called Antigens, while the liquid plasma contains matching defense proteins called Antibodies.

1. ABO Blood Group System

Depending on the antigens present on RBCs, human blood is divided into four groups:
• Group A: Has Antigen A on RBCs.
• Group B: Has Antigen B on RBCs.
• Group AB: Has both Antigens A & B.
• Group O: Has neither Antigen A nor B.

2. Rh Factor (Positive / Negative)

Along with A and B, RBCs may also have another surface protein called the Rh Antigen (D antigen):
• Rh Positive (Rh+): Rh antigen is present (~85–90% people).
• Rh Negative (Rh−): Rh antigen is absent.

3. What is Agglutination (Clumping)?

When blood is mixed with an antiserum containing matching antibodies (e.g., Antigen A + Anti-A serum), the antibodies bind the RBCs together into visible red granules or clumps. This clumping is called Agglutination.

Universal Donor — Group O−

O Negative (O−) RBCs have no A, B, or Rh antigens on their surface, so they do not trigger clumping in any recipient's body.

Universal Recipient — Group AB+

AB Positive (AB+) individuals have no Anti-A, Anti-B, or Anti-Rh antibodies in their plasma, so they can safely receive blood from any group.

1.4 Step-by-Step Laboratory Procedure

  1. 1 Label the Glass Slide: Take a clean, dry glass slide and mark three separate circles labeled A, B, and D (Rh).
  2. 2 Sterilize the Fingertip: Clean the tip of the left ring finger using a 70% alcohol swab and allow it to air-dry for a few seconds.
  3. 3 Prick with Sterile Lancet: Uncap a brand-new sterile lancet, gently prick the sterilized fingertip once, and wipe away the first tiny drop with dry cotton.
  4. 4 Place 3 Drops of Blood & Antisera: Place one small drop of blood into each circle (A, B, and D). Immediately add 1 drop of Anti-A (blue) to circle A, Anti-B (yellow) to circle B, and Anti-D (colorless) to circle D.
  5. 5 Mix & Observe Clumping: Mix each circle gently with a separate clean toothpick for 30 seconds. Rock the slide gently and observe within 2 minutes whether red clumps form or the liquid stays smooth.

1.5 Important Lab Precautions

  • ✓ Never reuse a lancet: Always use a fresh, sealed sterile lancet for each person and dispose of it in a sharps bin.
  • ✓ Use separate mixing sticks: Never use the same toothpick for Anti-A, Anti-B, and Anti-D circles, or cross-contamination will give a false result.
  • ✓ Do not let dropper tips touch blood: Hold antiserum droppers slightly above the blood drop.
  • ✓ Read within 2 minutes: Drying at the edges after 3–4 minutes can look like false clumping.
Biosafety Rule: Always perform finger-prick blood tests under your Class 10 Science teacher's supervision.
